In this post, we argue that the definition of empirical truth inherently implies
that perceived reality is not a cognitive bias[1].

We first define empirical evidence and assume that empirical evidence is used
to arrive at empirical truth[2]:

> A thing is evidence for a proposition if it epistemically supports this
> proposition or indicates that the supported proposition is true. Evidence is
> empirical if it is constituted by or accessible to sensory experience.

The important idea to note is that empirical truth needs sensory input,
i.e., it's not arriving at the truth using only the mind; it's using the body
and senses as well in order to arrive at the truth. Thus, it assumes that
perceived reality is not a cognitive bias[1].

We believe assuming perceived reality to be a cognitive bias is a central
assumption in texts like Advaita Vedanta[3], where the force of Maya is the
cosmic force that makes us believe that perceived reality is not a cognitive
bias. This assumption leads to a difference in how we define concepts like
sound[4][5] from the traditional scientific definitions.


Thus, we argue that the definition of empirical truth inherently makes an
assumption that is contrary to texts like Advaita Vedanta, i.e., it does not
characterize the perceived reality as a cognitive bias. We qualify that our
knowledge of this aspect of Advaita Vedanta is based on intuition and not
study.  Furthermore, we take a guess that the concept of empirical truth is what
distinguishes between Shruti and Smriti[6].
