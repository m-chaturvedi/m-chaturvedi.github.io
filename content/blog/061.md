In this post, we argue that the two ways of characterizing perceived reality
change the definition of sin.

As we have discussed in a previous post, perceived reality can be characterized
as a cognitive bias if the world is understood using only the mind[1]. We believe
that this fundamentally changes how sin is understood.

When perceived reality is not characterized as a cognitive bias, an individual
is represented by their physical body[2]. When this happens, we attach emotions to
sin, and sin is typically considered to be bad or immoral.

When perceived reality is considered a cognitive bias and the world is
understood as an abstraction using only the mind, and an individual is
represented by an abstraction, there cannot be any emotions attached to sin;
there can only be artifacts of the mind attached to it, i.e., knowledge or the
lack of it. Thus, sin is understood as acting in a state of ignorance, or lack
of knowledge. This, we believe, is why the Sanskrit word pāpa, when understood
from the point of view of Advaita Vedanta, is simply acting in ignorance, which
essentially has no emotions attached to it and is defined from the point of
view of the mind. We couldn't find an authoritative definition of sin, but
here's the best we found[3].

> Sin, in Vedantic terms, is essentially ignorance (avidyā) of one's true nature,
> which leads to inappropriate actions. It's not an inherent flaw or a stain on
> the person, but rather a misunderstanding that causes mental disturbance and
> unaligned behavior.

Thus, in this post, we argue why the definition of sin as characterized in
Vedantic terms is different from how it is characterized by other religions[4].
We note that our understanding is based on intuition rather than study.
