In this post, we discuss an aspect of natural languages, specifically English.
We also note that we only make observations about the written aspect of
languages, not of the spoken aspect.
We call this concept "word-distance" and define it similarly to how it is
defined in computer science [1]:

<i>Given two words, A and B, what is the distance between their meanings that
warrants their existence as separate words?</i>

We keep the metric of measuring distance vague, but believe there must be ways to quantify it using Mathematics.  We now define some common ways in which words with almost zero word-distance exist in languages like English:

1. _Context_: We observe that some languages like English have different words
   that exist for different contexts even when they have the same meaning. For
   example, the word _awesome_ in a casual context would be translated to
   _impressive_ in a more serious context, although the distance between them is
   only contextual.

2. _Synonyms_: Another way low word-distance can be introduced is via
   synonyms. Many times, synonyms can be used in the same context. For example,
   a synonym for _awesome_ can be _amazing_, and both can be used in a casual
   context.

3. _Time[2]_: Words can get out of fashion and become dependent on time, like _thrice_
   is no longer used; it is _three times_ now.

4. _Space[2]_: Even if the language used is the same, separation by space can create
   different new words for the same meaning. For example, in American English
   the word _tap_ is not used; it is called _faucet_.


For a language that describes Nature, like Math is for Physics, we believe that
it should be very clear what the word-distance between any two words A and B
is. And possibly, there should be some way to quantify meaning. For a language
for Physics, the only context is that of Nature, and synonyms just add noise to
the language, so the above-mentioned ways can be avoided. Additionally, using
different words at different times and in different places can add unnecessary
noise.

We note that our observations are only from the point of view of the mind and
may be valid for philosophy and science. From the point of view of the body and
senses, i.e. for poetry and emotional expression, we believe that the existence
of words that can be used interchangeably does provide colorfulness to the
language. We have previously written about a perfect language[2], sound[3], and
ether[4] that are related to this post.

Thus, we proposed an idea of word-distance based on meaning and made a few
observations as to how low word-distance words add noise in scientific and
philosophical contexts.
