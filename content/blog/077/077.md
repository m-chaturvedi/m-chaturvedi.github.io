In this post, we argue that two ways of characterizing perceived reality lead
to two different ways of thinking about language.

We have previously argued that perceived reality can be characterized as a
cognitive bias when understood only through the mind, but not when the body and
senses are used in addition to the mind[1][2][3].  We believe that this leads to two
different ways of thinking about language. We take the example of a pencil and
observe how characterizing perceived reality as a cognitive bias changes how we
think about a pencil.

Let’s define a pencil as follows, using Google Gemini’s definition for now:

> A pencil is a thin, pointed tool used for writing and drawing that features a
> solid marking core enclosed in wood or plastic.

Now, assume a person has never seen a traditional pencil, but knows all the
elements of this definition. They are then given a blackboard and a piece of
chalk in a plastic holder, like the following:

![alt](./chalk_in_plastic.jpg)

Clearly, this would fit the definition of a pencil. A language has two ways to
address this ambiguity, stemming from two different assumptions:

1. If perceived reality is a cognitive bias, objective reality is the reality
experienced in the state of mind, S, during deep meditation, which is similar
to deep sleep[3]. In this case, the perceived reality is the physical manifestation of
objective reality. Thus, a pencil is a physical manifestation of the
abstraction that represents a pencil. This leads to characterizing
plastic-covered chalk as a pencil as well.

2. If perceived reality is not a cognitive bias, it becomes objective reality.
   In this case, chalk covered in plastic may or may not be characterized as a
   pencil, like is the case with chalk. One might make the definition more
   specific, try to gain more context by discussing it with other people who
   use the language, or have reasons to coin another word for it or not, like
   spreading propaganda.

The first approach is common in computer science, where the definition of a
pencil is similar to defining an API that is disconnected from its
implementations. The second approach is common in natural languages such as
English. We speculate that, in a language used to describe nature, the first
approach leads to less noise because it stems from the assumption that
perceived reality is a cognitive bias[4].

Thus, in this post, we observed how two ways of thinking about perceived
reality change how we think about language.
